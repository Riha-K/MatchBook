01

From zero - what is an order book?

A stock exchange is a shop where people shout prices. The order book is the notebook of all standing offers. MatchBook is that notebook in C++ RAM.

Bid (buy)

Someone wants to buy. “I will pay at most 100.” Higher bid is more aggressive. Best bid = highest buy price.

Ask (sell)

Someone wants to sell. “I will take at least 101.” Lower ask is more aggressive. Best ask = lowest sell price.

Limit order

An order with a price cap/floor and a size. That is all MatchBook accepts. We do not implement market orders (“buy now at any price”).

When do they trade?

When best bid ≥ best ask. Buyer is willing to pay at least what the seller wants. That overlap is a match. A trade is the fill.

Price priority

Better price goes first. Buy 102 beats buy 100. Sell 99 beats sell 101.

Time priority

Same price: whoever arrived first fills first. That is why each price is a FIFO queue.

Partial fill

Buy 7 vs sell 10 → trade 7. Seller still has 3 resting. Leftover size is buy_remaining / sell_remaining.

Spread: if best bid is 100 and best ask is 101, nothing matches. The gap of 1 is the spread. Orders rest until someone crosses.

Elevator pitch (45-60 seconds)

“I built MatchBook, an in-memory C++ limit order book. Bids and asks live in price-ordered maps. Each price level is a FIFO queue, so better prices match first and equal prices follow arrival order. I implemented add, cancel, and match for limit orders, including partial fills. I replay a tick CSV as a simple back-test and use chrono to measure matching throughput and per-order latency. Matching is single-threaded, which is how most low-latency books avoid lock contention on the hot path.”

No bid yet. Nothing matches. Order rests.

Step 2 - buy 4 @ 100

Step 3 - buy 7 @ 101 (crosses)

Best bid becomes 101, which is ≥ 101. Match min(7,10) = 7.

TRADE buy=id3 sell=id1 px=101 qty=7 buy_remaining=0 sell_remaining=3 BIDS ASKS 100 : [id2 qty=4] 101 : [id1 qty=3] id3 fully filled (partial fill of the sell)

Step 4 - cancel sell id1

Index says id1 is a sell at 101. Scan that deque, erase it. Ask level 101 disappears.

BIDS ASKS 100 : [id2 qty=4] (empty)
Q: Where is the partial fill?
The sell wanted 10, only 7 traded. sell_remaining=3. The buy of 7 is fully filled so it is popped. That is a partial fill of the resting order.
Q: At what price do we trade?
This code sets t.price = ask_px (the sell price). In the demo both sides are 101 so it does not matter. If a buy at 105 hits a sell at 101, we print 101. Say: “I fill at the ask. A common exchange rule is maker/resting price. Mine is a simplification.”

07

Tech stack - why each piece

C++17

Pace language. Structured bindings not required. We use auto, steady_clock, STL.

STL map / deque

Enough to implement price-time priority without Boost or a custom tree. Interviewers can read it on a whiteboard.

CSV, not Postgres

Hot path is RAM. A database would add milliseconds. CSV is the market-data stand-in.

chrono

JD: profile and benchmark. We time the match call, not the whole process.

Not in v1 on purpose: Boost, Redis, FIX, Kafka, lock-free queues, GPU, floating prices, market orders, multiple symbols, networking. If they ask “would you add X?” - yes later, not in this repo.

Honesty - never invent this

Do not say: lock-free, FPGA, kernel bypass, 100 GbE, FIX gateway, nanosecond SLA, production exchange, multi-asset matching, official back-test with PnL.

Do say if asked: cancel scans the price-level deque so it is O(L). Fill price is the ask. One instrument, memory only. Benchmark is a laptop micro-benchmark.

On the resume - true

  • Price-ordered maps of FIFO queues
  • Add, cancel, match, partial fills
  • Throughput + per-order latency
  • Tick CSV replay as a simple back-test

Not in the code

  • Threads on the matcher
  • Network / market-data feed
  • Stop orders, iceberg, market orders
  • Persistence of the book
10

Likely questions

Q: Walk me through MatchBook.
Pitch + draw bids/asks + the 4-step demo. End with: “CSV replay and a 100k chrono benchmark live in main, not in the book.”
Q: Why map of queues, not one big heap?
Price and time are different keys. A heap of orders makes “best price” easy but cancelling and FIFO-at-same-price messy. Map keeps prices ordered; deque keeps arrival order inside a price.
Q: How does cancel work?
unordered_map gives side and price. I scan that deque for the id and erase. I did not store list iterators, so cancel is O(level size), not O(1). I would switch to list + iterator in the index if cancel became hot.
Q: Why not multithreaded matching?
The book is one object. Two threads adding would need a lock on every order and could reorder time priority. Low-latency shops usually give one symbol to one thread.
Q: How would you make it faster?
Integer prices already. Next: flatten top-of-book into arrays, object pool for orders, avoid allocating per add, maybe a custom price ladder. I would measure before rewriting - that is what the chrono loop is for.
Q: What is a tick?
A market event in time. In my CSV: timestamp, side, price, qty. I treat each as a new limit order.
Q: Design an order book on the board.
Two sorted maps, FIFO per level, id index, match while bid≥ask, partial fill with leftover qty. That is this repo.
Q: Difference vs ShelfLock?
MatchBook is in-memory matching, single-threaded, latency. ShelfLock is inventory + Postgres + threads because checkout must survive process restart and concurrent buyers.
